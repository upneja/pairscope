# Pairscope v2 Instrument Mapping

This document maps each assessment question to its source psychometric instrument, noting the specific items, subscales, and adaptations made for the Pairscope context.

---

## 1. Gottman Four Horsemen Questionnaire (FHQ)

Source: Gottman, J.M. (1999). *The Marriage Clinic: A Scientifically Based Marital Therapy.* W.W. Norton.

The FHQ is a self-report measure assessing the frequency of four destructive conflict patterns identified by Gottman's longitudinal research on couple interactions.

### Criticism Subscale

| Question ID | Pairscope Adaptation | FHQ Category | Adaptation Notes |
|------------|---------------------|--------------|-----------------|
| c1 | Focus on partner's character vs. situation | Criticism - Global blame | Simplified from FHQ's multi-item criticism scale. Captures the core distinction between complaint (specific) and criticism (global character attack). |
| c20 | Dishes scenario - language choice | Criticism - "You always/never" | Scenario format based on Gottman's observation coding of complaint vs. criticism. Tests whether respondent uses specific complaint language or generalizing criticism. |
| f1 | Kitchen-sinking past grievances | Criticism - Historical buildup | Targets the "kitchen-sinking" pattern (bringing up accumulated grievances). Adapted from FHQ supplemental items. |
| f2 | Social embarrassment scenario | Criticism - Character attribution | Tests whether respondent attributes partner behavior to character flaw vs. situation. Based on Gottman's attribution research. |
| f3 | Use of "always/never" language | Criticism - Generalizing | Directly measures frequency of absolutist language, a key behavioral marker of criticism per Gottman's SPAFF coding. |

### Contempt Subscale

| Question ID | Pairscope Adaptation | FHQ Category | Adaptation Notes |
|------------|---------------------|--------------|-----------------|
| c2 | Forgotten task scenario | Contempt - Sarcasm/superiority | Scenario presents three response options ranging from contemptuous (eye-rolling, sarcasm) to healthy complaint. Based on Gottman's contempt markers. |
| c13 | Thinking partner is foolish | Contempt - Superiority/disrespect | Targets the cognitive component of contempt (viewing partner from a position of superiority). Adapted from FHQ contempt items. |
| f4 | Mocking/mimicking partner | Contempt - Mockery | Targets behavioral contempt (mimicry, hostile humor). One of the strongest SPAFF-coded contempt indicators. |
| f5 | Feeling smarter/more capable | Contempt - Superiority belief | Targets the attitudinal foundation of contempt. Gottman identifies this belief as the "sulfuric acid" of relationships. |
| f6 | Partner shares unrealistic idea | Contempt - Dismissiveness | Tests in-the-moment contemptuous dismissal vs. respectful engagement with partner's ideas. |

### Defensiveness Subscale

| Question ID | Pairscope Adaptation | FHQ Category | Adaptation Notes |
|------------|---------------------|--------------|-----------------|
| c3 | Explaining away partner's concern | Defensiveness - Excuse-making | Core defensiveness item measuring the reflexive "it's not my fault" response to partner complaints. |
| c29 | First reaction to being upset with | Defensiveness - Counter-attack ideation | Measures the cognitive precursor to defensive counter-attacks. Distinguishes from criticism (c1, c20) by focusing on the respondent as the one being addressed. |
| f7 | Cross-complaining (tit-for-tat) | Defensiveness - Cross-complaint | Targets the specific defensive behavior of immediately returning a complaint when one is received. Gottman's RCISS coding category. |
| f8 | Plumber scenario - response to valid complaint | Defensiveness - Accepting responsibility | Directly tests the ability to accept responsibility vs. deflect. Scenario design ensures the complaint is clearly valid. |
| f9 | Difficulty saying "I'm sorry" | Defensiveness - Repair failure | Targets inability to accept influence, which Gottman identifies as a strong predictor of relationship failure (especially in men). |

### Stonewalling Subscale

| Question ID | Pairscope Adaptation | FHQ Category | Adaptation Notes |
|------------|---------------------|--------------|-----------------|
| c4 | Response to overwhelm during arguments | Stonewalling - Withdrawal behavior | Scenario distinguishes between healthy self-regulation (taking a break with stated return) and stonewalling (silent withdrawal). |
| c23 | Emotional checkout while present | Stonewalling - Emotional disengagement | Targets the internal experience of stonewalling -- being physically present but emotionally absent. Key distinction from defensiveness. |
| f10 | Physical symptoms during conflict (DPA) | Stonewalling - Diffuse Physiological Arousal | Measures Gottman's DPA concept (heart rate > 100bpm, flooding). DPA is the physiological driver of stonewalling behavior. |
| f11 | Partner perception of going cold | Stonewalling - Observable withdrawal | Uses partner-perception framing to capture the interpersonal impact of stonewalling. Distinguishes from self-perceived "needing space." |
| f12 | Flooded and partner wants to talk | Stonewalling - Repair vs. retreat | Tests whether respondent uses structured self-soothing breaks (Gottman's recommendation) or unstructured withdrawal. |

### Positive Indicators

| Question ID | Pairscope Adaptation | Gottman System | Adaptation Notes |
|------------|---------------------|----------------|-----------------|
| c5 | Knowing partner's biggest worry | Love Maps Questionnaire | Adapted from Gottman's Love Maps card deck. Tests depth of cognitive knowledge about partner's inner world. |
| c10 | Listing admirable qualities | Fondness & Admiration System | Based on the Fondness & Admiration Questionnaire. Tests whether the positive sentiment override is intact. |
| c16 | Phone distraction scenario | Turning Toward vs. Away | Based on Gottman's "bids for connection" observation coding. Scenario measures response to a low-stakes bid. |

## 2. Experiences in Close Relationships - Revised (ECR-R)

Source: Fraley, R.C., Waller, N.G., & Brennan, K.A. (2000). An item response theory analysis of self-report measures of adult attachment. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 78*(2), 350-365.

The ECR-R is a 36-item self-report measure of two fundamental dimensions of adult attachment: anxiety and avoidance.

### Anxiety Subscale (ECR-R Items 1-18)

| Question ID | Pairscope Adaptation | ECR-R Items Referenced | Adaptation Notes |
|------------|---------------------|----------------------|-----------------|
| c7 | Worry about reciprocity of care | Items 1, 5 | Combines the concern about partner's investment (Item 1: "I'm afraid I will lose my partner's love") with comparative caring (Item 5: "I worry my partner doesn't care as much"). |
| c12 | Ruminating about partner thinking of you | Items 2, 14 | Adapted from Item 2 ("I worry about being abandoned") and Item 14 ("I worry a lot about my relationships"). Reframed as specific cognitive behavior. |
| c21 | Needing post-conflict reassurance | Items 6, 8 | Based on Item 6 ("I need reassurance that I am loved") and Item 8 ("I resent it when my partner spends time away"). Focuses on the repair/reassurance-seeking cycle. |
| f13 | Fear of being replaced | Items 4, 18 | Adapted from Item 4 ("I worry about being alone") and Item 18 ("My partner makes me doubt myself"). Targets abandonment-specific anxiety. |
| f14 | Phone-checking during separation | Items 10, 16 | Behavioral marker adapted from Item 10 ("I need constant reassurance") and Item 16 ("My desire for closeness scares people away"). Reframed as observable behavior. |
| f15 | Partner seems distant scenario | Items 6, 8 | Scenario format targeting hyperactivation of the attachment system. Tests anxious interpretation of ambiguous partner behavior. |

### Avoidance Subscale (ECR-R Items 19-36)

| Question ID | Pairscope Adaptation | ECR-R Items Referenced | Adaptation Notes |
|------------|---------------------|----------------------|-----------------|
| c8 | Discomfort with emotional closeness | Items 19, 23 | Adapted from Item 19 ("I prefer not to show a partner how I feel deep down") and Item 23 ("I feel comfortable depending on romantic partners" - reversed). |
| c15 | Not sharing deepest feelings | Items 25, 27 | Based on Item 25 ("I tell my partner just about everything" - reversed) and Item 27 ("I usually discuss my problems and concerns with my partner" - reversed). |
| c26 | Response to partner's distress | Items 21, 31 | Scenario adapted from Item 21 ("I feel comfortable sharing thoughts and feelings") and Item 31 ("I don't mind asking partners for comfort" - reversed). Tests behavioral avoidance in high-emotion context. |
| f16 | Comfort with emotional distance | Items 29, 33 | Targets deactivating strategies. Adapted from Item 29 ("I get uncomfortable when a partner wants to be very close") and Item 33 ("It helps to turn to my partner in times of need" - reversed). |
| f17 | Compulsive self-reliance | Items 35, 36 | Based on Item 35 ("I turn to my partner for many things" - reversed) and Item 36 ("I find that my partner doesn't want to get as close as I would like" - reversed). Targets defensive self-sufficiency. |
| f18 | Future conversation scenario | Items 25, 27 | Scenario targeting intimacy avoidance in response to relationship deepening. Tests whether closeness attempts trigger deactivation. |

## 3. Big Five Inventory - Short Form (BFI-K)

The BFI-K is a 21-item short form of the Big Five Inventory. Pairscope uses three of the five dimensions most relevant to relationship functioning.

| c25 | Lingering mood after setbacks | Items 4, 14 | Based on Item 4 and Item 14 ("Can be moody"). Measures emotional recovery speed in relationship context. |

| c28 | Following through on promises | Items 3, 13 | Based on Item 3 and Item 13 ("Is a reliable worker"). Adapted to measure dependability within the relationship. |

### Agreeableness Subscale (BFI-K Items 2, 7, 12, 17)

## 4. Couples Satisfaction Index (CSI-4)

Source: Funk, J.L., & Rogge, R.D. (2007). Testing the ruler with item response theory: Increasing precision of measurement for relationship satisfaction with the Couples Satisfaction Index. *Journal of Family Psychology, 21*(4), 572-583.

The CSI-4 is a 4-item measure of relationship satisfaction with strong psychometric properties (IRT-derived, high sensitivity to change).

| Question ID | Pairscope Adaptation | CSI-4 Item | Original Wording | Adaptation Notes |
|------------|---------------------|-----------|------------------|-----------------|
| c17 | Global satisfaction rating | Item 1 | "Please indicate the degree of happiness, all things considered, of your relationship" | Simplified wording. Original uses a 7-point scale from "Extremely Unhappy" to "Perfect." |
| c22 | Frequency of positive sentiment | Item 2 | "In general, how often do you think that things between you and your partner are going well?" | Minor wording simplification. Original 6-point scale adapted to 7-point for consistency. |
| c27 | Relationship happiness | Item 3 | "Our relationship is strong" | Reframed from agreement statement to happiness framing. Captures the same construct (positive relationship evaluation). |
| c31 | Warmth and comfort (mode_a only) | Item 4 | "I have a warm and comfortable relationship with my partner" | Near-verbatim. Present tense only meaningful for current relationships. |
| c32 | Relationship reward (mode_a supplemental) | Supplemental | "How rewarding is your relationship with your partner?" | Added based on Rogge's extended CSI items to improve reliability for mode_a respondents who get 2 extra items. |

**Scoring note:** The original CSI-4 uses different response scale widths per item. In Pairscope v2, all items use a uniform 7-point likert for UX consistency. Scores are remapped to the original CSI-4 scaling (Item 1: 0-6, Items 2-4: 0-5) during computation to preserve the validated 0-21 composite range.

## 5. Chapman's Five Love Languages Framework

Source: Chapman, G. (1992). *The Five Love Languages: How to Express Heartfelt Commitment to Your Mate.* Northfield Publishing.

Chapman's framework identifies five primary ways people express and receive love. While not a psychometric instrument in the traditional sense, the forced-choice format mirrors Chapman's original Love Language Profile questionnaire structure.

| Question ID | Love Language Pair | Adaptation Notes |
|------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| c6 | Quality Time vs. Words of Affirmation | Contrasts undivided attention (QT) with verbal appreciation (WoA). Both are "emotional presence" languages but differ in modality. |
| c11 | Physical Touch vs. Acts of Service | Contrasts physical comfort (PT) with practical help (AoS). Tests sensory vs. instrumental love reception. |
| c18 | Gifts vs. Quality Time | Contrasts thoughtful material expression (G) with shared experience (QT). Tests whether the respondent values tangible tokens or shared time. |
| c24 | Physical Touch vs. Words of Affirmation | Contrasts nonverbal comfort (PT) with verbal comfort (WoA). Both are "comforting" languages but differ in modality. Tests preference under emotional distress. |
| c30 | Gifts vs. Acts of Service | Contrasts surprise material expression (G) with practical acts (AoS). Both are "action" languages but differ in symbolic vs. functional value. |

**Coverage matrix (each language appears in 2 forced-choice pairs):**

| Language | Appears In |
|----------|-----------|
| Words of Affirmation | c6, c24 |
| Quality Time | c6, c18 |
| Physical Touch | c11, c24 |
| Acts of Service | c11, c30 |
| Gifts | c18, c30 |

This balanced design ensures each language has equal opportunity to be selected, preventing sampling bias. The 5-question forced-choice design produces a rank ordering of all five languages with minimal respondent burden.

## 6. Discriminant Validity Notes

### Criticism vs. Contempt
- **Criticism** items focus on generalizing complaints ("you always/never") and character attacks.
- **Contempt** items focus on superiority, mockery, sarcasm, and disgust.
- Key discriminator: Criticism attacks behavior with global language; contempt attacks the person from a position of moral superiority.
- Items c1/c20 (criticism) test complaint-broadening. Items c2/c13 (contempt) test disrespect and superiority.

### Defensiveness vs. Stonewalling
- **Defensiveness** items focus on counter-attacking, excuse-making, and refusing responsibility.
- **Stonewalling** items focus on emotional withdrawal, shutdown, and disengagement.
- Key discriminator: Defensiveness is an active response (fighting back); stonewalling is a passive response (shutting down).
- Items c3/c29 (defensiveness) test active deflection. Items c4/c23 (stonewalling) test withdrawal and emotional absence.

### Attachment Anxiety vs. Neuroticism
- Both involve negative emotionality, but anxiety items are relationship-specific (fear of abandonment, reassurance-seeking) while neuroticism items are broader emotional patterns applied to relationship contexts.
- Items c7/c12/c21 (anxiety) focus on partner-specific worry. Items c9/c25 (neuroticism) focus on general emotional reactivity that happens to manifest in relationships.
